Disk Scheduling & I/O

Devices are slow, diverse and unpredictable. The OS hides their differences behind uniform interfaces and arranges requests so the hardware is used efficiently.

Talking to devices

TechniqueHow it worksTrade-off
PollingCPU repeatedly checks a device's status registerSimple, low latency for fast devices; wastes CPU on slow ones
InterruptsDevice signals the CPU when readyCPU does useful work meanwhile; each interrupt has overhead
DMAA controller moves whole blocks between device and memoryCPU involved only at start and finish; ideal for bulk transfers
Memory-mapped I/ODevice registers appear as memory addressesUniform load/store programming model

The kernel's I/O subsystem adds buffering (smoothing speed differences), caching (keeping hot data in RAM), spooling (queueing output for exclusive devices such as printers) and device drivers that translate generic requests into device-specific commands.

With asynchronous I/O a program starts a request and keeps running, and the OS reports completion later (usually through an interrupt or callback). With synchronous (blocking) I/O the process waits until the request is done.

How a hard disk spends its time

Access time = seek + rotational latency + transfer. Seek time (moving the head to the right cylinder) is by far the largest term, a few milliseconds. Rotational latency averages half a revolution (about 4.2 ms at 7200 RPM). Transfer of a block is tiny by comparison. So the OS reorders requests to minimize head movement.

Disk-scheduling algorithms

AlgorithmRuleStrength / weakness
FCFSServe in arrival orderFair, but lots of back-and-forth movement
SSTFNearest request nextLow movement; can starve distant requests
SCAN (elevator)Sweep to one end, then reverseNo starvation; favors the middle cylinders
C-SCANSweep one way; jump back and sweep againMore uniform waiting time
LOOK / C-LOOKLike SCAN/C-SCAN but turn around at the last request, not the disk edgeSlightly less movement

Try it: load the textbook queue (head at 53) and confirm FCFS = 640 and SSTF = 236 cylinders. Then use Random queue several times. Does SSTF always beat FCFS? Is SCAN ever worse than SSTF? Increase the queue near the head and imagine a request at cylinder 199 waiting. That is the starvation risk.

Beyond spinning disks

  • SSDs have no seek time. Their schedulers care about parallelism across flash channels, and the controller handles wear leveling, garbage collection and write amplification. Simple “none” or deadline schedulers are common.
  • RAID combines disks for speed or reliability: RAID 0 stripes (fast, no redundancy), RAID 1 mirrors, RAID 5 stripes with distributed parity (survives one failure), RAID 6 survives two.
  • Block layer merges adjacent requests and reorders them before they reach the device. Linux's mq-deadline, bfq and none are examples.

AdvancedGoing deeper: how storage really behaves
  • Command queues. SATA drives accept up to 32 outstanding requests (NCQ) and reorder them in firmware, and NVMe supports up to 65,535 queues of 65,536 commands. The disk-scheduling algorithms on this page matter less when the device itself knows the geometry better than the OS does.
  • The I/O scheduler on flash. With no head to move, Linux uses the none or mq-deadline scheduler on SSDs and NVMe devices. SCAN and C-SCAN remain useful mainly for spinning disks.
  • Write amplification and TRIM. Flash cannot overwrite a page; it must erase a whole block first. The SSD’s translation layer remaps writes and later garbage-collects, which is why writing a byte can cost far more than a byte internally, and why the OS tells the drive which blocks are free (TRIM).
  • RAID and what it does not do. Striping gives speed, mirroring gives redundancy, and parity (RAID 5/6) trades capacity for tolerance. RAID is not a backup: it protects against a failed disk, not against deletion, corruption or fire.
  • DMA and the IOMMU. A device transfers data directly to memory without the CPU copying it. An IOMMU gives each device its own address translation, so a buggy or malicious device cannot read arbitrary memory. This is the page-table idea applied to hardware.
  • Polling returns. At millions of operations per second, interrupts themselves become a cost, so io_uring and user-space NVMe drivers poll queues instead. The textbook trade-off between polling and interrupts reverses as devices get faster.
